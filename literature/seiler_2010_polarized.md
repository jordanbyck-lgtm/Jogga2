# Seiler 2010 — Polarized training

A foundational review article. Seiler synthesizes training-log data from elite endurance athletes (XC skiing, rowing, running, cycling) and finds a consistent intensity distribution pattern that has come to be called "polarized" or "80/20" training.

## Key findings

- Across multiple elite endurance sports, athletes spend ~80% of training time at low intensity (below first lactate threshold, LT1) and ~20% at high intensity (at or above lactate threshold, LT2).
- Time spent in the **moderate zone** (between LT1 and LT2) is intentionally minimized in elite training. This zone produces fatigue without optimal stimulus.
- The 80/20 distribution holds across athletes, sports, and seasons — it's a robust observation.
- Performance gains in trained athletes are larger from polarized distributions than from "threshold-heavy" or "pyramidal" distributions in head-to-head comparison studies.

## Operational implications

- **Measure by time, not distance.** Easy miles take longer; distance-based ratios understate easy share.
- **Easy must be genuinely easy.** Heart rate below ~75% of max, RPE conversational, no metabolic stress.
- **Hard sessions must be hard.** Threshold and above. Don't water down quality work to make it more "comfortable."
- **The middle zone is the trap.** "Comfortably challenging" daily running is the most common failure mode for self-coached recreational runners.

## Caveats

- Replication in **recreational** populations is partial. Studies in trained but non-elite runners generally support the framework, but with smaller effect sizes and more individual variability.
- Sub-20 mpw runners may not have enough total volume for 80/20 to be meaningfully different from 70/30 or 90/10. The polarized framework is more meaningful at higher volumes.
- "Polarized" in Seiler's framework has 3 zones (Z1/Z2/Z3 in his terms — easy / threshold / VO2). This is sometimes confused with consumer-watch 5-zone schemes.

## How the coach uses this

- Anchor the `polarized_80_20` strong guideline.
- Combat the "moderate run drift" common in self-coached athletes.
- Justify why "slow down on easy days" is not a soft suggestion but a structural training principle.
